1. What are you actually selling?
I'm sure your product has tens of amazing features. As a builder, it's easy to get lost in the cool stuff you've built (I do too) - but if you want people to actually buy your product, you need to think from their perspective.
What does your ideal customer want? What is their desired transformation? What is the underlying human need they are trying to address?
Every purchase comes back to one of eight human desires. Drew Eric Whitman calls them the Life-Force 8 in Ca$hvertising:
To stay alive, feel well, and live longer
To enjoy food and drink
To be free from fear, pain and danger
To find a partner
To live comfortably
To be better than the people around you
To look after the people you love
To be liked and respected
Those eight sell more than every other human want combined. So answer two questions before you write anything:
What problem are you really solving?
What changes for this person afterwards?
That painful moment is your ad, and that change is what you are selling.
Let's work through the rest of this together with a case study.
Take a fictional calorie tracking app for women called Sona. We'll use this as a working example and build everything together.
Our fictional app has some amazing features: logs food from pictures, scans barcodes, counts macros, syncs to your watch, charts your week and can even order food for you.
But none of them really matter. What matters, is the following:
The problem/pain we are solving: Standing on the scale after six weeks of eating well and still seeing the same number
What changes for this person afterwards: She feels better and healthier. She's more confident, and people treat her differently for it, like they like her more and respect her more. She can go to the beach without being ashamed of her body.

2. Research what already works
If you skip this part, you will make a good looking video that doesn't convert, and you'll conclude that "AI ads don't work". They do. But you did not do the work.
Your competitors already paid to find out what converts. That information is public.
The Meta Ads Library
is free and you can find any competitor running ads in any country. The rule of thumb is that a long-running ad is usually a winning ad. Nobody keeps paying to serve a loser. It is not always true, but it is a good proxy.
For this research we look at one of the biggest apps in the category, Cal AI.
As of 9 August 2026 they have roughly 650 live ads in the library. I took the top 25 by impressions, pulled the videos and transcribed all of them.
Every one of those 25 started on 13 or 14 April 2026 and is still running four months later, in three to nine variants each. By the rule of thumb above, all of them are working.
Six formats carry the entire account:
The bet: someone on the street is asked to guess the calories in their food. "If you guys can guess how many calories are in your food, I will pay for it."
Before and after: a body, then what he did to get there. "Look, the only way you guys are going to make a transformation like this is if you're in a calorie deficit."
It's not your fault: "You're not overweight because you eat too much, you just have no idea how much you're actually eating."
A 17 year old built it: "A 17 year old just changed the fitness industry by making it so easy to track your calories."
The news clip: a real Fox Business segment about the app, cut into an ad.
No talking. Someone eats, the number appears over the food, and nobody says anything at all.
Four things repeat in all of them:
No hook is about the product. Every opener is a person, a bet, a physique or an accusation. The app never appears before second eight.
The mechanic is said out loud, in almost the same words every time: "You take a picture of your food and it tells you the calories and macros." That is give-away-the-secrets, proven with somebody else's money.
They promise less work, not more weight: "No typing, no mental math." Nobody promises a number of pounds.
They answer the obvious doubt with a number. The doubt is whether a photo can really know what is on your plate. "FDA food labels have 80% accuracy, this app has 90%."
They do a really good job at reframing the problem their customer is facing. The pain is not that tracking is hard. It is that you have been blaming yourself for what was actually a measurement problem.
"It's not because they're lazy, but because they're guessing".
Every strong ad in the set does a version of it.
Now do it on your own market. Open the Meta Ads Library, find your competitors, and actually watch twenty or thirty of their ads with a pen next to you.
For each one, write down the first line word for word, the pain it goes after, the objection it answers, and the format it uses.
By about the fifteenth ad the same three or four pains will keep coming back, and those are the ones your market has already validated with real money.
You do not need to reinvent the wheel. Take the best script you found, and either say it better than they do, or say it from a person they are not using.

3. Learn how an ad is built
You don't need a marketing degree to write good ads. You need a bit of common sense and to be willing to learn what a good ad is. Plenty of books go deep on this and for this article, we'll use the framework outlined by Alex Hormozi' in
$100M Leads
.
A good ad has three parts: the call out, the value, and the call to action.
The call out
The opener stops the right person and lets everyone else scroll. Hormozi calls it the cocktail party effect, the way you hear your own name across a loud room. A call out aimed at everybody stops nobody.
He puts about eighty percent of the outcome on this alone. If the first two seconds fail, nothing after them is ever seen.
The call out is not just the first line. It is the line, the way it is said, and what is on screen while it is said, and all three of those land in the same two seconds.
The line names a person. It hands the viewer their own situation, so it has to be about them rather than about you.
You need a new call out for every ad you make. Hormozi gives four shapes you can use, and you have already seen all four running on Cal AI's account.
Labels name the group out loud, like "women over 35" or "new mums".
Yes-questions ask something they have to agree with: "You eat well all week and you still can't work out why nothing happens".
If-then statements: "If you've been eating well for six weeks and the scale hasn't moved, it isn't your body."
Ridiculous results are an outcome big enough that it needs explaining, like "Her salad had more calories than a Big Mac."
The visuals and sounds in your ad are also part of the call out.
Six of Cal AI's live ads have no script in them at all, just someone eating with the number appearing over the food.
The bet ads open on a plate held out to a stranger before anybody has spoken, and the transformation ads open on a body. In all three cases you know what the ad is about before the first word.
The value
What they get, and the more specific you are the better it works.
Say exactly how it works, out loud. Hormozi calls this giving away the secrets and selling the implementation.
Bad: "It just knows what you're eating."
Good: "You just snap a picture of your food and it instantly tracks your calories and macros for you."
Cal AI uses the good one in almost every ad in the set that has a script in it. A company at that spend does not repeat the same sentence twenty times by accident.
Then answer one objection.
There are five reasons somebody doesn't buy. They don't need it, can't afford it, aren't in a hurry, don't want it enough, or don't believe you.
Here are two examples of how Cal AI handles objections in their ads:
They don't believe you: "While FDA food labels have 80% accuracy, this has 90% accuracy."
They don't think they need it: "That's why most people aren't losing weight. It's not because they're lazy, but because they're guessing their diet."
Pick the one your buyer most likely has and answer that one properly, rather than gesturing at all five and landing none of them.
The call to action
The entire point of the ad is that somebody does something at the end of it, so the last line has to tell them what that something is, in the words they would use themselves.
Cal AI closes almost every ad with the same two part move.
"So if you're serious about getting in shape, stop guessing and start tracking the smart way."
"If you're serious about locking in with your diet, use the link below and download CalAI."
The first half qualifies the viewer, so anyone it is not for can leave without clicking. The second half names the app and points at the tap.
The winning script, taken apart
The best written ad in the set is the reframe, and every piece of this part is in it. It's about 106 words in about thirty seconds, which is the pace you should be writing at.
You're not overweight because you eat too much, you just have no idea how much you're actually eating. Like be honest, you guess your calories and you forget snacks and by the end of the day you're way off. That was literally me until I found this. It's called CalAI. You just snap a picture of your food and it instantly tracks your calories and macros for you. No typing, no guessing, no effort, it made dieting stupid simple. Like now I actually know what I'm eating and it shows. So if you're serious about getting in shape, stop guessing and start tracking the smart way.
The call out. "You're not overweight because you eat too much, you just have no idea how much you're actually eating."
The problem. "Like be honest, you guess your calories and you forget snacks and by the end of the day you're way off."
The value, with the implementation given away. "You just snap a picture of your food and it instantly tracks your calories and macros for you. No typing, no guessing, no effort."
The objection, which is trust. "Like now I actually know what I'm eating and it shows."
The call to action. "So if you're serious about getting in shape, stop guessing and start tracking the smart way."
Thirty seconds is enough room for those three parts to stretch into five beats. The call out is the first one. The value takes the middle three, because you have the time to name the problem, show the mechanic and hand over a piece of proof. The call to action is the last.
Hook → Problem → Demo → Proof → CTA
Every ad on that account worth watching runs on those five.
Our version
We keep the pain, the reframe and all five beats, and change four things.
Who says it. Their creators are men in gyms and strangers stopped in the street. Ours is one woman at her own kitchen table at night.
What's in front of her. They open on a plate held out to a stranger. We open on one dinner taken completely apart into five small bowls with a scale in the middle.
One number, for trust. We're two months in. Cal AI never gives a weight figure, so neither do we.
The product name. Everything else stays where it is, including the order of the beats.
Six stages, thirty seconds, 112 words.
Hook. "You're not overweight because you eat too much. You just have no idea how much you're actually eating."
Problem. "Like be honest, you're guessing your calories, you forget the snacks you had standing at the fridge, and by the end of the day you're way off."
Turn. "That was literally me, until I found this. It's called Sona."
Demo. "You just take a picture of your food and it works out the calories and the macros in about four seconds. No typing, no guessing, no effort."
Proof. "Now I actually know what I'm eating, and two months in, it shows."
CTA. "So if you're serious about getting in shape, stop guessing and start tracking the smart way."
Get this one working before you make any others.
Once it does, the variants are cheap: the same script with a different woman in a different room, with a different hook or answering a different objection.
4. Why Seedance 2.5 for this
Why is this model such a gamechanger for UGC ads?
Go back to the ads we pulled apart. Nineteen of those twenty five run longer than fifteen seconds.
The median is twenty one, and nine of them run past twenty five. Fifteen seconds was the cap before this model. So a thirty second ad meant making it in two halves and joining them, and the second half never quite matched the first.
Thirty seconds in one go. The whole ad is one generation now, so the woman at the end is the same woman as at the start.
The voice is better. Plenty of models talk now. This one holds the same voice, in sync, for the full thirty seconds.
The physics are better. She picks things up, puts them down, eats. It gets that right far more often than the models before it.
You can steer it. Write out six stages and it works through them in order. Which means we can be very specific in how we write our prompts and guide the model really well.
5. Prompt anatomy
We've done the research and wrote our script. Now we need to think about the scenery and how to prompt it correctly.
There are two ways to do this: you can prompt the video model directly, or you can generate a storyboard first with all the different scenes.
I always generate a storyboard first, because it's cheaper and faster to iterate over images until you have your desired results.
Do I like this woman, or does she look like an ad? Is the room right, or is it too tidy and too styled? Is she holding the thing she's meant to be holding?
By the time the board looks right, every creative decision has already been made and approved.
There's a second reason too. The board is a reference you feed forward, which also ensures you get better results in your generated video by reinforcing what you want both in the prompt and in the reference image.

The video prompt
There's no official guide to writing one of these, so I made one.
I read about 3,000 posts across 150 searches. I read the ByteDance documentation, which contradicts a good half of them. It says negative constraints only work for subtitles and music, while on X it seems the people getting the best results write long ban lists anyway.
So I ran close to a hundred generations to find out who was right.
A good Seedance 2.5 prompt has twelve blocks. Write them as ALL-CAPS labels with plain sentences underneath.
The start and the end of a prompt count for more than the middle. So the references and the camera go first, and the bans go last.

CAMERA: You say what it is filmed on, who is holding it, and how it moves. If you want it to look like UGC, this is also where you ask for the flaws by name, like shake, drifting frames, hunting focus and uneven light. Seedance defaults to clean and steady if you let it, so you need to prompt the little flaws that make it look natural into it.
LOOK: You write the light, the colour and the grain as things you could point at. The skin clauses live here too:
realistic skin texture, visible pores around the nose and cheeks, natural slight unevenness, no filter quality
no doll skin, no porcelain skin, no airbrushed skin, no skin blur, no glossy plastic finish
STYLE: You set how it is performed, in our case, she talks on camera in every shot her face is in, and the voiceover only runs over the one insert where she is not on screen. You write that here, again on every stage, and again in CONSTRAINTS. If you drop one of the three, about half your takes come back as narrated b-roll.
VOICE: You cast the voice the way you cast the face, with pitch, pace, accent and speech habits, then the sound you do not want.
CHARACTER: You give the age, the build, the hair, the clothes and three or four things about the face.
SETTING: You describe the room. In our case, we'll add a bit of a mess to make it look more natural. You name every place the ad visits and ban the rest, or the model invents one. You can skip this block when your stages already describe the space.
STAGES: This is the body of the ad, one beat at a time. Each beat gets one change and an end state, because the end state is what the model steers toward. For a 30 second video, you write 110 to 120 words. Then you size each window to the line inside it, at 15 words per four seconds. Mine run 5, 7, 3, 7, 4, 4.
BRAND: You say what gets spoken out loud, who says it, and how to pronounce it. You never say what gets shown, because Seedance mangles text and always will.
AUDIO: You name the sound effects as physical events, cast the voice, and make a decision about music. For example:
[SOUND] Strictly only naturally occurring sound and foley, no music allowed.
CONSISTENCY: You list what cannot change between cuts, which is the face, the clothes, the props, the counts and the geography. You write the counts as numbers, because those drift first. Anything you leave out here is free to move, and across six cuts it will.
CONSTRAINTS: This is the ban list and it always goes last. This is a list I've compiled after making tens-hundreds of generations.

To summarize:
Work out what you actually sell. Not the feature but the moment it hurts, and the Life-Force desire sitting underneath it.
Spend an hour in the Meta Ads Library. Your competitors already paid to find out what converts, and their winners are public. Take the best one and either say it better or say it from a person they are not using.
Write the ad on a structure that already works. Call out, value, call to action, laid out as hook, problem, demo, proof and CTA.
Generate the storyboard before the video. An image comes back in seconds for cents and tells you whether the idea looks right, which is a much cheaper way to find out than a video is.
